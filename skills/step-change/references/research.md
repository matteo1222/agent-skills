# Research behind the step-change strategy

## Conclusion

*The Science of Scaling* is best encoded as a **strategy generator**, not as a
claim that every ambitious target should become a promise. Its useful core is
to select one outcome whose scale and intentionally compressed horizon make the
current playbook fail, use that frame to filter the present system, and search
for a qualitatively different leverage mechanism. Goal research supports
specific challenging goals, feedback, and strategy search, but makes extreme
goals conditional on capability, commitment, resilience, and available slack.

The `step-change` skill therefore adds **Proof** to the book's
Frame → Floor → Focus framework. Proof converts a dramatic reframing into a
proximal learning loop and keeps three different time claims separate: design
horizon, evidence-backed forecast, and approved commitment.

## The practitioner framework

Hardy and Erickson define:

- **Frame:** what the goal makes visible. Their operating version is a 10x
  outcome on an accelerated timeline, large enough to require a different
  model rather than more of the same.
- **Floor:** what the goal filters out—activities, offers, paths, or standards
  that cannot contribute at the new scale.
- **Focus:** what remains worth pursuing—the few high-leverage paths and
  partners that might realize the goal.

The publisher summarizes the same logic as setting an impossible goal, using
time as a strategic filter, eliminating what cannot scale, and building a
simple system. The authors' company describes Frame, Floor, and Focus as an
unreasonable 10x target, a raised execution baseline, and the highest-leverage
moves. These are first-party descriptions of the method, not independent
evidence that 10x or three years is an optimal prescription.

## What goal research supports

Locke and Latham's review of 35 years of goal-setting research finds that
specific, difficult goals generally outperform vague “do your best” goals.
Important moderators include commitment, self-efficacy, feedback, task
complexity, and access to effective strategies. On novel, complex tasks,
performance goals can interfere with learning; specific learning goals and
proximal feedback can work better until an effective strategy is discovered.
This supports the skill's learning-goal and Proof steps.

Source:

- [Locke and Latham, 2002, *Building a Practically Useful Theory of Goal
  Setting and Task Motivation*](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.57.9.705)

## Where stretch goals fail

Sitkin and colleagues define stretch goals as seemingly impossible given
current capabilities. Their contingency theory predicts the best chance of
benefit when recent performance and slack resources are both high: success
supports confidence, while slack allows repeated experiments and failed trials
to be absorbed. Organizations with weak performance and little slack are most
likely to reach for stretch goals and least able to benefit; the paper
recommends small wins or bounded learning instead. It also warns that visible
success stories overrepresent survivors.

Ordóñez and colleagues catalogue additional side effects of aggressive goal
systems: attention can narrow around the measured target, risk preferences can
distort, learning and intrinsic motivation can fall, and ethical shortcuts can
become more attractive. These risks are strongest when a target is imposed as
pressure without guardrails and monitoring.

Seijts and Latham report that a challenging learning goal outperformed a
performance-outcome goal on a complex business simulation. Ariely and
Wertenbroch found that binding deadlines can reduce procrastination, but their
evenly spaced external deadlines outperformed the self-set schedules; the study
does not support “shortest possible” as a general rule. Holton and colleagues
found that goal commitment can reduce sensitivity to valuable alternatives,
which supports scheduled alternative scans and kill criteria.

## Operational rules derived from the evidence

1. Apply the framework to one chosen mission; an extreme goal is a poor tool
   for deciding between unrelated values.
2. Treat the impossible scale and compressed time as a design horizon while
   searching for leverage.
3. Protect safety, legality, ethics, relationships, and explicit obligations
   from the filtering step.
4. Require slack or bound the experiment so failure is absorbable.
5. Use a learning goal when the work is complex and the strategy is unknown.
6. Require proximal feedback and an explicit expand, revise, or stop rule.
7. Track counter-metrics and scan alternatives at review points.
8. Keep personnel, capital, and production changes staged until the evidence
   and decision authority justify them.
9. Promote the horizon to a forecast only after evidence; promote it to a
   commitment only after owner approval.

No source establishes a universally correct multiplier, deadline, or
Frame → Floor → Focus package. Those elements remain practitioner heuristics
whose value should be judged by whether they produce a safer, simpler,
testable, and genuinely nonlinear path.
